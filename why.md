# Why Resetly works this way

Resetly is a wellness app with no streak, no score, no account, and no chart of
how you have been feeling. Every one of those absences was a decision, and each
one cost something. This page is the reasoning behind them, written down,
because a list of missing features is not an explanation.

## What this was a reaction to

A lot of software in this space is built around the same handful of parts: a
number that goes up, a run of consecutive days you can break, a large library
to browse, and an account to create first. Those are all reasonable things to
build. They just did not fit the thing being attempted here, and a big library
works against it directly, because the moment you might actually want one short
thing to do is not a good moment to choose between four hundred of them.

So Resetly is what was left after taking all of that out.

## One question, one answer

Resetly asks how a moment feels, optionally what it is about, and then offers
**one** short thing you might try. Not a ranked list, not a category to browse.
One.

The rule written down at the start was that the product answers a single
question — *what is one small thing I can do right now* — and everything that
made that question harder to answer got cut, including features that would have
looked good in a screenshot.

You can run the thing it offers, swap it for something else, or close the app.
Nothing is required, and closing the app is not recorded as a failure, because
it isn't one.

## No streak, and nothing that counts your absence

There is no streak, no day counter, no "days since your last check-in", and no
gap in any list where an absence would show.

The reason is narrow, and worth stating plainly: a person who did not open this
app for two weeks has not failed at anything. Once a count exists, missing a day
starts to mean something, and a fortnight where you had other things going on
becomes a small mark against you inside an app you opened for the opposite
reason. Nothing in Resetly counts your absence, anywhere, and that is the
reason.

Fewer people opening the app more honestly is the better outcome of the two.

## No chart of your feelings

If you want, you can note how intense something felt before a reset and after
it. Two numbers, one to ten, entirely optional.

What the app will never do is plot them. No graph, no trend line, no average,
no moving score. A line chart of ten-point self-reports over time reads as a
measurement of a person, and it isn't one — it is a series of guesses you made
on eleven different days in eleven different moods about what a "6" means.

This one went wrong first and had to be fixed. The screen that looks back at
your record originally reported those numbers as a pair of averages. It read
like an instrument reading, and on a record where someone had rated everything
the same it degraded into a very confident sentence that said nothing at all.
It ships
now as three plain counts — how many times the second number was lower, the
same, or higher — printed alongside how many check-ins that came from, so you
can see the size of the thing you are looking at.

Nothing in that screen is labelled better, worse, improving, or concerning. It
counts what is there and says so, and when there isn't enough yet to count, it
says that instead of inventing something.

## No account, and nothing leaves the phone

There is no sign-up, no login, no server, and no sync. What you record sits in
storage on your device. All of it can be deleted from Settings in one step.

This is less a feature than the only arrangement that seemed defensible. Nobody
should have to open an account to write down that they are having a rough
afternoon.

The same reasoning shaped a smaller decision. Where the app needs to know that
you are an adult, it asks you to confirm it rather than asking for your date of
birth, because a date of birth would be a new piece of personal data collected
from you, and collecting a piece of personal data in order to support a claim
about collecting very little is not much of a trade.

## The app is free, and the ads are walled off

Resetly is funded by advertising, and how that works is better explained than
left to assumption.

The part of the app that shows ads and the part that records your check-ins are
built as separate systems, with no path between them: what you record is not
used to choose an ad, and is not passed to the advertising side of the app at
all. That is a description of how it is put together rather than a statement of
good intentions, which is the only version of this claim worth making.

The binding version of all of this is the [Privacy Policy](privacy) — that is
the document to read if you want the complete and current account of what is
recorded and where it goes. This page explains the thinking; that one is the
commitment.

There are places an ad is simply not permitted to appear: while you are
entering how you feel, at any point during a reset, on the timer, and anywhere
in the support screen. That last one is enforced in the ad layer itself rather
than left to the design of the screen, because a rule that depends on nobody
ever making a mistake in a layout file is not a rule.

The one surface that was genuinely hard is the screen that reflects your record
back at you, where every other element is a statement about *you*. An ad
borrowing that voice would read as a recommendation derived from your emotional
history. So the ad there is built to look like a visibly different kind of
object, and it stays a single one.

## The small stuff, because that is where it actually shows

The subtitle in the App Store says *One small reset, if you want*. It used to
say *when you need it*. It was cut for two reasons: it ran three characters over
Apple's limit, and it told the reader they had a need. The app knows nothing
about you. "If you want" assumes neither a need nor a wish — it says the thing
is there and leaves the judgement where it belongs, which is the way the app
talks everywhere else.

When you stop a reset early, the record says **Stopped early**. Not
*incomplete*, not *abandoned*. It is a fact about what happened, and it is not
a verdict.

Every line of copy in the app is checked against a list of phrases it is not
allowed to use — clinical vocabulary, diagnostic framing, anything that tells
you what you should do about yourself, and the whole family of monitoring words
like *average*, *trending*, *improving*, *your levels*. It is checked
automatically, on every change, because no one keeps a rule like that by hand
across a year of small edits.

## What this app is not

Resetly is a general wellness and self-reflection app. It does not provide
medical advice, diagnosis or treatment, and it is not a substitute for
professional care. It cannot tell you what is going on with you, and it is
built specifically so that it never pretends to.

If you are in crisis, please contact your local emergency services or a crisis
line in your country.

## Why write this down

None of the above is visible from a screenshot. What a screenshot shows is an
app with less in it than the ones beside it, and that reads as thinness unless
you know it was deliberate.

It was deliberate. If it sounds like something you would use, Resetly is on the
App Store. If it doesn't, that is an entirely reasonable conclusion — better
skipped than downloaded and found disappointing.
